Article #28
Anxiety and depression disorders in oncological patients under palliative care at a hospital service: a cross-sectional study
Gustavo Souza Gontijo Garcia, Karina Cardoso Meira, Alessandra Hubner de Souza, Nathalia Sernizon Guimarães
Brazil (Barbacena, Minas Gerais), published 15 August 2023 — BMC Palliative Care, 22, article 116
70 patients with advanced cancer, Hospital Ibiapaba Oncology Sector, outpatient palliative care clinic (prospective cross-sectional study, data collected October 2020 - April 2021, during the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil; 78 invited, 70 accepted = 10.2% refusal loss)

Instruments:
Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) — Portuguese-validated version; 14 items (7 anxiety, 7 depression); cutoffs: 0-7 mild, 8-14 moderate, 15-21 severe; HADS ≥8 = considered to have depression or anxiety
EORTC QLQ-C30 (version 3.0) — 30 items; Global Health/QoL scale; Functional scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, social function); Symptom scales (fatigue, pain, nausea/vomiting) + 6 single items (dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, diarrhea, financial difficulty); items scored 0-100; higher score = better QoL/functionality on functional scales, but higher score = worse symptom burden on symptom scales

variables:
sociodemographic ->
age, sex

clinical ->
tumor type/aggressiveness (classified as "more aggressive": rectum, pancreas, kidney vs. "less aggressive": prostate, breast), primary cancer site (colon, breast, lung, rectum, stomach, pancreas, esophagus, unknown, ovary, prostate, melanoma, nasopharynx, bladder, larynx, CML, oropharynx, penis, kidney)

EORTC QLQ-C30 domains/symptoms ->
Overall Performance (Global Health/QoL), Total Function, Physical Function, Cognitive Function, Emotional Function, Social role, Financial difficulty, Dyspnea, Pain, Fatigue, Insomnia, Appetite loss, Nausea/Vomiting, Constipation, Diarrhea

outcome variables ->
Depression (HADS-D ≥8, yes/no)
Anxiety (HADS-A ≥8, yes/no)
Depression and/or Anxiety combined (yes/no)

Overall prevalence:
Depression: 44.3% (95% CI 32.7-55.9%)
Anxiety: 25.7% (95% CI 15.5-35.9%) [note: abstract states 25.7%, Discussion text states 28.5% — inconsistency present in the original article]
Depression and/or Anxiety combined: 52.9% (95% CI 41.20-64.6%)

Interpretation of ORs: since EORTC functional-scale ORs are calculated per 10-unit INCREASE and higher functional scores = better functioning, OR<1 for Physical/Total Function/Overall Performance means better functioning REDUCES odds of anxiety/depression (protective). Key findings for model training:
- Functional status (Overall Performance, Physical Function, Total Function) is a consistent, statistically robust PROTECTIVE factor against depression, anxiety, and combined depression/anxiety across both univariate and adjusted models — one of the more consistently reproduced findings across this corpus (echoes Article 25/Bužgová Karnofsky findings and Article 30 Bangladesh PPS findings)
- Fatigue is the only individual symptom that reached significance for depression alone (adjusted OR 1.22) and for the combined outcome (adjusted OR 1.21) — contrasts with Article 27 (Delgado-Guay), where fatigue frequency did NOT differ significantly by mood status; this discrepancy across studies is worth flagging in your model documentation as tool/population/definition-dependent (ESAS frequency ≥1 vs. EORTC continuous severity score are different measurement approaches)
- Nausea/vomiting and loss of appetite reached significance specifically for the COMBINED depression/anxiety outcome, not for either disorder alone in the adjusted model — suggests these symptoms may be markers of comorbid/more severe affective burden rather than either disorder individually
- Emotional function (an EORTC subscale) shows a striking POSITIVE association with depression/anxiety odds in the univariate analysis (OR 1.32-1.55) — this is counterintuitive only until you note that in EORTC scoring, a higher "Emotional Function" score conventionally indicates BETTER functioning; the authors' consistent positive OR direction across all three outcomes suggests either a scoring/coding inversion in their analysis or a construct-overlap artifact (HADS and the EORTC emotional function subscale may be capturing overlapping constructs) — flag this specific table as needing cautious interpretation if used for feature engineering, since the direction contradicts the expected clinical relationship
- No significant association found between age, sex, or tumor aggressiveness/type and any of the three outcomes in this sample — contrasts with several other articles in your corpus (e.g., Article 21 Ethiopia found age >64 a strong risk factor for anxiety; Article 26 Hong Kong found age negatively correlated with distress) — reinforces that demographic risk factors are inconsistent across settings/cultures and should likely be modeled with wide credible intervals or country/context as an interaction term
- High combined depression/anxiety prevalence (52.9%) sits at the upper end of the range compared to most of this corpus, though the authors note it is comparable to Azevedo et al. Brazil (42% depression) and higher than Manchester/UK (22% depression) or China (11% depression) comparators — again reflects setting/population/instrument heterogeneity
- Authors explicitly flag a potential COVID-19 period effect (data collected during Brazil's second pandemic wave) that may have inflated psychological distress — a temporal/contextual confounder relevant if your training corpus spans pre- and post-pandemic studies
- Cited comparator: a Chinese study found 61% of symptomatic advanced cancer patients received NO treatment for their symptoms, with only ~2% receiving psychotropic medication for mental suffering — reinforces the cross-study pattern of psychological under-treatment in palliative oncology, relevant context for ACOMPAÑAR's alert/escalation logic

Limitations noted by authors:
- Cross-sectional design — outcome (anxiety/depression) and independent variables (QoL, symptoms) collected simultaneously, so temporality/causality cannot be established
- Small sample size (n=70) from a single hospital service in Brazil — limits generalizability and precision of subgroup estimates (wide confidence intervals evident throughout)
- Data collection coincided with the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil — possible period effect increasing measured psychological distress
- Some numeric inconsistencies within the original article itself (anxiety prevalence reported as both 25.7% in abstract/Table 1 and 28.5% in Discussion text) — treat anxiety prevalence figure with caution or verify against source if precision matters
