Symptoms of Advanced Cancer in Palliative Medicine: A Longitudinal Study
Leslye Rojas-Concha, Maiken Bang Hansen, Morten Aagaard Petersen, Mogens Groenvold
Palliative Care Research Unit, Department of Geriatrics and Palliative Medicine, Bispebjerg and Frederiksberg Hospital, University of Copenhagen, Copenhagen, Denmark (Groenvold additionally affiliated with the Department of Public Health, University of Copenhagen). Published 2023 — BMJ Supportive & Palliative Care, Vol. 13, pp. e415-e427, doi: 10.1136/bmjspcare-2021-002999.
201 patients with advanced cancer admitted to specialized palliative care in Denmark; longitudinal design (assessment at admittance and again during the first month of follow-up); mean age 66 years; 48.3% female; mixed cancer types (distribution not specified in available sources).

Patient characteristics/covariates used to examine association with symptom change (specific list not confirmed from available sources — likely includes age, sex, cancer diagnosis, and performance status based on this research group's standard methodology)

variables:

Prevalence (per source review table, Deng et al. 2026, Table 1): Depression (HADS-D) prevalence in this sample: 10%. Sample: n=201, mean age 66, % female 48.3%, mixed cancer types.
NOTE: 10% is the lowest depression prevalence in this 9-study corpus (range ~10-88%). This may reflect a stricter/higher HADS-D cutoff, a less advanced-stage or better-functioning sample relative to other studies, or a genuine difference in the Danish palliative care population. The exact cutoff and sample severity characteristics could not be confirmed from available sources — flag for verification before using as a benchmark figure.

Key findings for model training:
- This is the only explicitly LONGITUDINAL study in the corpus with symptom trajectory (not a single cross-sectional snapshot) as its central design feature, examining how depression/symptoms change over the first month after admittance to palliative care — directly relevant if ACOMPANAR needs to model depression as a trajectory rather than a static state.
- The study's framing (symptomatology broadly, with depression as one symptom among several) differs from studies that treat depression as the sole primary outcome — useful reminder that depression often co-occurs with and is measured alongside other physical/psychological symptoms in real clinical assessment protocols.
- The very low prevalence (10%) relative to the rest of the corpus is a notable outlier at the opposite extreme from Park et al. (88%) — the two studies together illustrate how much reported depression prevalence can vary by cutoff, population, and clinical setting, which is a caution against treating any single prevalence figure as a universal benchmark.
- Danish register-based/clinical palliative care research from this group (Rojas-Concha, Hansen, Petersen, Groenvold) tends to be methodologically rigorous and large-scale (national registers), so this study's findings may be more generalizable within a Danish/Scandinavian palliative care context than smaller single-center studies elsewhere in the corpus.

Limitations (inferred / reported):
- Full text not accessible for this extraction — the exact symptom battery, complete covariate list, exact HADS-D cutoff, and detailed statistical results on symptom change could not be verified independently; entry is based on abstract-level objectives and secondary-source data only.
- As with other studies in palliative care settings, attrition over the one-month follow-up (due to death or clinical deterioration) is a plausible methodological limitation, though this could not be confirmed from available sources.
- Single-country (Denmark) setting — cultural and health-system-specific factors may limit direct comparability with studies from other countries in this corpus.
