Article #29
Depression—A Major Contributor to Poor Quality of Life in Patients With Advanced Cancer
Kjersti S. Grotmol, Hanne C. Lie, Marianne J. Hjermstad, Nina Aass, David Currow, Stein Kaasa, Torbjørn Å. Moum, Alessandra Pigni, Jon Håvard Loge — on behalf of the European Palliative Care Research Collaborative (EPCRC)
International multi-center study (17 centers, 8 countries — includes Norway, Italy, UK, Switzerland, Germany, Austria; largest single-nationality subgroup Norwegian), published 2017 — Journal of Pain and Symptom Management, Vol. 54, No. 6, pp. 889-897, doi: 10.1016/j.jpainsymman.2017.04.010
563 patients with advanced cancer (final analytic sample with complete data), drawn from the European Palliative Care Research Collaborative–Computerized Symptom Assessment Study (EPCRC-CSA), a cross-sectional study; recruitment 2008-2009 from in/out-patient units, hospices, inpatient palliative care beds, general oncology, and medical wards; original eligible convenience sample n=1051, 12 excluded for severe cognitive impairment (MMSE<18), 476 excluded for missing data, leaving n=563 (53.6% of eligible sample) with complete datasets

Instruments:
EORTC QLQ-C30 Global Health/QoL scale — 2 items (overall health, overall QoL past week), 8-point numerical rating scales (0=very poor, 7=excellent), transformed to 0-100 score, higher=better; Cronbach's α=0.83 in this sample
Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) — 0 (dead) to 100 (normal functioning)
Modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) — systemic inflammation biomarker composite: 0 = CRP≤10mg/L; 1 = CRP>10mg/L; 2 = CRP>10mg/L AND albumin<35g/L
Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS) — used for nausea, loss of appetite, breathlessness; 0-10 scale (0=no symptom, 10=worst possible); NOTE: ESAS psychological/pain/well-being items deliberately excluded from analysis due to content overlap with depression/QoL outcome
Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) — single item "pain at its worst during last 24 hours," 0-10 scale
Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) — self-report; DSM-5-aligned; 0-3 per item (not at all/several days/more than half days/nearly every day); KEY METHODOLOGICAL CHOICE: authors used only the 5 NON-SOMATIC items (depressed mood, anhedonia, feelings of worthlessness/guilt, poor concentration, thoughts of death/self-harm), excluding the 4 somatic items, to avoid artificial inflation of the depression-QoL relationship due to symptom overlap with advanced cancer itself; modified score range 0-18 (referred to as "depression symptom severity"); Cronbach's α=0.79 in this sample
Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) — cognitive screening, used only for exclusion criteria (severe impairment <18 excluded)

variables:
sociodemographic ->
age, gender, nationality (Norwegian vs. not), marital status (married/de facto vs. not)

clinical/medical status ->
primary cancer diagnosis (gastrointestinal tract, pulmonary, breast, male genital organs/prostate, other), current disease status (metastatic vs. loco-regionally advanced), care setting (in-patient vs. out-patient), total comorbidities (heart disease, arthritis, COPD, renal disease, liver disease, others)

treatment ->
current treatment type (chemotherapy only, other oncological treatment [radiotherapy ± chemo, hormone therapy, other anti-tumor treatment], none), opioid use (yes/no)

prognostic factors ->
KPS score, mGPS (0/1/2), self-reported 6-month weight change, CRP, albumin

symptom burden (ESAS/BPI, 0-10 scale) ->
nausea, loss of appetite, breathlessness, worst pain intensity (last 24h)

outcome variables ->
Global Health/QoL score (EORTC, 0-100)
Depression symptom severity (modified PHQ-9, non-somatic items only, 0-18)

Prevalence: Major depression (DSM-5 criteria approximated via non-somatic PHQ-9 items) in this sample: 11%. Comparable meta-analysis benchmark (structured clinical interviews): 14.3% (95% CI 11.1-17.9%). General population benchmark: 5-6% depression, mean QoL ~75 (vs. 50.5 in this advanced cancer sample).

Key findings for model training:
- Depression severity was THE SINGLE STRONGEST PREDICTOR of QoL in this large, rigorously controlled multivariate model — outweighing systemic inflammation (mGPS), physical performance (KPS), and all measured physical symptoms (pain, breathlessness, appetite loss, nausea) combined in terms of single-block explanatory power (5.8% unique variance vs. next-largest single predictor KPS at 2.0%)
- This is one of the methodologically strongest studies in your corpus for establishing depression as an INDEPENDENT driver of poor QoL — not merely a byproduct of physical symptom burden or poor prognosis — because the model explicitly controls for systemic inflammation biomarkers (mGPS/CRP/albumin), functional status (KPS), symptom burden (ESAS/BPI), and treatment type simultaneously
- Critical methodological insight for your own algorithm: the authors deliberately excluded SOMATIC depression symptoms (fatigue, sleep, appetite items) from their depression measure specifically because these overlap with cancer symptoms and would artificially inflate the depression-QoL association — this is directly relevant to how ACOMPAÑAR's algorithm should handle the DSM-5/clinical dimension, since a naively-summed symptom score risks conflating disease burden with depression severity. Consider building parallel "somatic" and "cognitive/affective" sub-scores rather than one blended depression score.
- Demographic variables (age, gender, marital status) were NOT significant predictors of QoL in either univariate or multivariate models — authors explicitly note this contradicts general-population findings where demographics do predict QoL, and interpret it as disease severity "superseding" demographic influence in advanced/palliative populations. This is a valuable, well-powered (n=563) counterpoint to smaller studies in your corpus that did find demographic effects (e.g., Article 21 Ethiopia age effect, Article 26 Hong Kong age effect) — sample size and case-mix homogeneity (all advanced/incurable) may explain why demographic signals wash out here specifically
- Not receiving opioids was significantly associated with WORSE QoL at earlier model steps (β=-0.18 to -0.25) but lost significance once symptom burden and depression were added — suggesting opioid access/undertreated pain partially mediates its effect on QoL through the pain/symptom pathway rather than being independently protective
- Weight change (6-month) was significant in univariate analysis but dropped out entirely once prognostic factors and later blocks were added — a caution against using weight loss as a standalone predictor without controlling for inflammation/functional status
- Selection/attrition bias explicitly documented: patients included (complete-data sample) were significantly LESS symptomatic than those excluded — lower CRP, higher albumin, lower pain, lower depression severity — meaning true population-level depression prevalence and symptom burden in advanced cancer is likely UNDERESTIMATED by this study's 11% depression figure; the authors note a companion analysis on a different subset of the same larger EPCRC-CSA cohort found higher depression prevalence
- This is a large (n=563), international (8 countries), methodologically rigorous, prospectively-designed reference study — well-suited as an anchor/benchmark dataset for calibrating your model's expected variance-explained ceiling (~29-30% from clinical variables alone) before adding relational/demographic/religiosity dimensions unique to ACOMPAÑAR's 5-dimension framework

Limitations noted by authors:
- Cross-sectional design — cannot establish causality between depression and QoL (bidirectional relationship plausible)
- Psychosocial variables such as social support NOT included in the model — a known QoL contributor omitted here, directly relevant to ACOMPAÑAR's "relational" dimension which could add explanatory value beyond this model's 29% ceiling
- Common-method variance possible (self-report measures for both depression and QoL), though mitigated by including objective/observer-rated indicators (KPS, biomarkers)
- Depression measured via self-report screening tool (PHQ-9), not structured diagnostic interview — though PHQ-9 corresponds well to SCID gold-standard criteria and is ASCO-recommended
- Selection bias: most severely affected patients (highest symptom burden, most impaired) were disproportionately excluded due to missing data — likely underestimates true prevalence/severity relationships in the sickest patients
- Data collected 2008-2009 — treatment landscape may have shifted somewhat since, though authors argue advanced/incurable disease trajectory dynamics are unlikely to have changed substantially
